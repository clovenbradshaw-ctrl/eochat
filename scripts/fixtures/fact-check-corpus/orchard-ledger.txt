THE ORCHARD LEDGER OF PENNANT ROW
Kept by Aldous Quennell, steward

The old orchard behind Pennant Row holds 214 trees, of which 96 are the
russet variety the parish calls Thornapple and the rest a late green cider
apple of no settled name. The steward's ledger begins in the spring the
frost took the blossom, and runs for thirty-one seasons without a gap.

Quennell records that the Thornapple crop failed entirely twice: once to the
frost, and once to a blight he attributes to standing water at the foot of
the slope. In the best season the orchard yielded 41 hogsheads of cider, and
in the worst none at all. The average across the ledger is 23 hogsheads.

Grafting was done in the second week of March by a man named Tobias Reed,
who came up from the coast each spring and took his fee in cider rather than
coin. Reed's grafts are marked in the ledger with a small cross. Of the 96
Thornapple trees, 58 carry his mark.

The ledger's last entry is a note that the western hedge wants laying, and
that the steward has bought a scythe blade for one shilling and fourpence.
Nothing after that is written in Quennell's hand. A later hand, unnamed,
records the sale of the orchard to the parish.

The cider press stood in a stone building at the north corner. It was worked
by two men and a horse, and could handle the fruit of forty trees in a day.
The building is now roofless, and the press screw was sold for scrap.
